How Kanchipuram Silk Sarees Are Made: From Raw Silk to Finished Weave

Understanding the process behind a Kanchipuram silk saree — from raw silk thread to the finished korvai-woven piece — makes the price tag, and the craft, make a lot more sense.

By Kanchi360 Editorial · Published 27 Jan 2026 · Updated 27 Jan 2026 · 8 min read

It Starts With Raw Silk and Zari Preparation

Before any weaving begins, raw mulberry silk thread is processed, twisted and dyed to the required colours, while zari thread — traditionally silver wire coated with gold, or a tested alloy substitute — is prepared separately to the specification the saree calls for. Getting the dye colour consistent across the full length of thread needed for one saree is itself a skilled, careful process.

Setting Up the Handloom

Setting up a handloom for a new saree design, especially one with an elaborate border or pallu pattern, is a labour-intensive process on its own, involving arranging thousands of individual warp threads in the correct sequence before a single pick of the weft can be woven. For intricate designs, this setup step alone can take a significant amount of time before actual weaving even starts.

Did You Know: A single elaborate silk saree with heavy zari work can take anywhere from several days to a few weeks of dedicated handloom weaving, depending on the complexity of the border and pallu design.

The Korvai Technique: Weaving Body and Border Separately

What makes Kanchipuram sarees structurally distinctive is the korvai technique, where the body of the saree and the border are often woven with different coloured silk on essentially two interlocking sets of shuttles, joined together at the point where border meets body using a special interlocking join. This is a demanding technique requiring real skill and coordination, since a poorly executed korvai join is either weak or visually uneven — which is exactly why it's one of the things to check when buying, as covered in our buying guide.

Weaving the Pallu

The pallu — the decorative end-piece of the saree — is often the most elaborate part of the weave, sometimes taking as much time on its own as the rest of the saree body, particularly for bridal-weight pieces with dense zari motifs.

The pallu is often the most intricately woven part of the saree

Finishing and Quality Checks

Once woven, the saree is cut from the loom, checked for any weaving defects, and finished — including trimming loose threads and, in some cases, a light wash or pressing before it reaches the showroom floor. Quality-conscious weavers and cooperatives inspect the border join, zari consistency and overall weave tightness at this stage.

  • Raw silk and zari thread preparation happens before weaving even starts
  • Loom setup for an elaborate design can itself take considerable time
  • The korvai technique — weaving body and border separately then interlocking them — is the signature Kanchipuram method
  • The pallu is frequently the most time-intensive part of the whole weave
  • Finishing and quality checks happen after the saree comes off the loom, before it reaches a shop
Is every Kanchipuram saree hand-woven?

Traditional, GI-recognised Kanchipuram silk sarees are handloom-woven, though power-loom imitations using similar visual patterns do exist in the broader market — this is exactly why checking for a GI tag and understanding the korvai join matters when buying.

Why does an elaborate saree take so much longer to weave than a simple one?

Complexity in the border and pallu design requires more intricate management of the zari and coloured threads throughout the weave, along with more careful, time-consuming korvai joining — all of which adds significant weaving time compared to a simpler, plain-bordered saree.
